Voice Enrollment (Speaker Recognition)

Voice Enrollment (Speaker Recognition)

Section titled “Voice Enrollment (Speaker Recognition)”

Status: Beta. The Enroll My Voice page and its navigation entry are marked Beta in the web UI. Enrolling and matching both require the on-demand ASR MicroVM speech engine, which is itself experimental.

Off by default, and off unless you deliberately turn it on. A voiceprint is biometric data about a person. EnableVoiceEnrollment defaults to false, and while it is false the enrollment table, the consent log, the portal route and the matching code path do not exist in the deployment at all — not merely hidden, not deployed.

Turning it on creates obligations that are yours, not this project’s. Biometric data is separately regulated in many jurisdictions (Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, the EU GDPR’s Article 9, and others), typically requiring informed written consent before collection, a published retention schedule, and deletion on request. This feature is built to make those obligations possible to meet — it captures consent explicitly and keeps an append-only record of it — but deciding that you may lawfully collect voiceprints from the people in your meetings is a decision for you and your legal counsel.

What it does

Section titled “What it does”

Speaker separation can tell voices apart but not who they are, so a conference-room transcript reads Other Participant (spk_0). Naming the voices lets you fix that by hand, and finding the names automatically reads the transcript for introductions.

Both of those start from zero in every meeting. Voice enrollment lets somebody record a short voice sample once, with their explicit consent, so that afterwards they are labelled by name from their first word in every meeting — including meetings where nobody introduced themselves.

The consent model

Section titled “The consent model”

The hard part of this feature is not the matching. It is that a voiceprint is biometric data about a person, and letting anybody else assert “they agreed” on their behalf would make the consent record worthless.

So consent is first-party and self-service. Nobody can enroll anybody else, and there is no administrative action that creates a voiceprint:

  1. An LMA user opens Enroll My Voice (marked Beta) in the navigation (or goes straight to /#/voice-enrollment) and is shown the consent terms in full, including what is stored, what it is used for, how long it is kept and how to withdraw.
  2. They consent by an explicit affirmative action — a checkbox they must tick plus a button they must press. There is no pre-ticked box and no implied consent from continuing.
  3. Only then does the portal offer to record voice samples.

Enrollment is limited to people who already have an account in the deployment. Somebody attending from another company cannot currently enroll, because doing so would mean giving them an account here; see limits we hold deliberately.

Consent is recorded before any audio is captured, so a record exists even if the person abandons the recording step. Declining is a normal outcome: the portal is a dead end with nothing stored.

What is stored, and where

Section titled “What is stored, and where”

Two separate tables, for two different reasons.

VoiceEnrollmentTable — the current state, and the only place biometric data lives:

FieldPurpose
SubjectIdThe enrollee’s Cognito sub, rather than an email, so the key carries no contact details.
DisplayNameThe name to label transcripts with, as the enrollee typed it.
CentroidThe voiceprint: a vector of floats. This is the biometric.
Statuspending, active or revoked.
SampleCount, EnrolledAt, ConsentExpiresAtOperational metadata.

VoiceConsentLogTable — an append-only history, holding no biometric data at all:

FieldPurpose
SubjectId (PK)Who the record is about.
RecordedAt (SK)ISO timestamp, so the history is ordered and unique per event.
ActionGRANTED, RE_CONSENTED, REVOKED, EXPIRED, DELETED.
TermsVersion, TermsSha256Which terms text, and its exact hash. Both are absent where the row records no terms — an admin revoking for somebody who never enrolled. An absent field means “not recorded”; a row never asserts terms it has no basis for.
ActorSub, ActorEmailThe authenticated identity that performed the action.
SourceIp, UserAgentWhere from.

The split is the point. Revoking consent deletes the voiceprint but keeps the record that consent was given and then withdrawn — which is exactly what an auditor needs and exactly what the person asking to be forgotten should not have to leave behind.

The audit trail

Section titled “The audit trail”

Three properties make the log an audit trail rather than a logfile:

It is append-only in IAM, not by convention. The application role is granted dynamodb:PutItem and dynamodb:Query on the consent log and is not granted UpdateItem or DeleteItem. The application cannot rewrite its own history even if its code is wrong or compromised. Only an operator with separate privileges can.

The terms are pinned by hash, and the hash is compared. TermsSha256 is the SHA-256 of the exact text the person was shown, stored both in the consent log and on the enrollment itself. An active enrollment stops being used for matching, and the portal asks its owner to agree again, as soon as either the TermsVersion or the text behind it changes. Previous consents still prove what was agreed to.

Comparing the text and not only the version matters because the retention period appears in the terms: the text says how many days a consent stands. So changing VoiceEnrollmentRetentionDays changes what people agreed to, and everybody is asked to agree again at the new period — without an operator having to remember to bump VoiceEnrollmentTermsVersion as well. Until they do, their voiceprints are not used; they are not deleted, and agreeing again and re-recording restores them. Plan a retention change accordingly: it asks every enrolled person to repeat the enrollment, so it is not a parameter to tune casually once people have enrolled.

Point-in-time recovery is on, so the log can be restored to any moment in the retention window.

Both tables survive stack deletion (DeletionPolicy: Retain), and that cuts both ways, so it is worth being explicit. It means a stack deleted by accident, or replaced, does not take the consent history with it — an audit record that a cdk destroy can erase is not much of an audit record. It also means deleting the stack leaves the voiceprints behind, in a table nothing is managing any more. If you are decommissioning a deployment and people have enrolled, delete those two tables deliberately after the stack, or ask people to withdraw first, which destroys the voiceprints and leaves only the log.

Who can enroll

Section titled “Who can enroll”

Anyone with an account in the deployment can enroll their own voice, and nobody can enroll anybody else’s. Enroll My Voice appears in the navigation for every user when EnableVoiceEnrollment is true.

There is no administrative enrollment action, and that is a property of the design rather than a gap in the UI: a consent somebody else gives on your behalf is not consent. An admin can revoke on somebody’s behalf — for a person who has left — which is recorded with the admin as the actor.

People without an account in the deployment cannot currently enroll. The obvious use for this feature is the participant from another company or the contractor in a meeting room, and they are exactly who it does not yet serve, because enrolling would mean issuing them an account here. See limits we hold deliberately.

Enrolling a voice

Section titled “Enrolling a voice”

The portal records a short, fixed script — enough speech for a stable voiceprint, which in practice is about 20–30 seconds across several utterances. The audio is streamed to an ASR MicroVM session in embed mode, which returns one embedding per utterance; those are averaged into the stored centroid.

The session is acquired for the enrollment and released as soon as it finishes, including when it fails. It is the same engine and the same short-lived MicroVM a meeting uses, so enrolling requires EnableMicrovmAsr=true: the embedding model lives in that image and Amazon Transcribe exposes no speaker embedding. In a deployment with enrollment on but that engine absent, the portal says so rather than telling somebody their recordings were unusable.

The sample audio is not kept. It is held in memory for the length of the request and never written to S3 or to a table. What persists is the centroid, which is a one-way summary: it is enough to recognise the voice and not enough to reconstruct the speech.

How a match is used

Section titled “How a match is used”

When a meeting starts in a deployment with enrollment on, the transcription session is seeded with the active centroids it is allowed to use, each labelled with its DisplayName. From then on the engine assigns speech to those names directly instead of minting spk_0.

A match is written as the segment’s speaker, in place of the voice id the segment would otherwise have carried. It is not currently marked as a match rather than a name somebody typed, and it is not offered for confirmation the way a name inferred from the transcript is. Correcting one is therefore the same job as renaming any other speaker, from the Speakers panel on the meeting page.

Nothing is named on a guess. A seeded voice is only ever assigned speech whose similarity score clears the engine’s threshold — it is never the nearest remaining option when a speaker cap is reached, and it does not consume cap room, because a deployment having more enrolled people than a meeting has participants is the normal case.

Which voices a meeting is given

Section titled “Which voices a meeting is given”

A meeting cannot know who is in the room. A transcript captured from a browser tab or a desktop app has no attendee list — that is the whole reason the Speakers panel offers a pasted one — so a starting session is given every enrollment that consent currently permits, up to 32 of them. An enrollment counts only while all five of these hold: it is active, it has a voiceprint, its TermsVersion is the one in force, the hash of the terms it agreed to matches the terms in force, and its consent has not lapsed. The transcriber asks for the list at the start of every meeting and keeps no copy, so a withdrawal applies to the very next meeting.

A voiceprint that is sent and never speaks does nothing. Matching is a similarity comparison against the voices actually heard, and a score below the threshold produces no name, so sending somebody’s centroid to a meeting they are not in produces no label for them.

Where more than 32 enrollments are permitted, the most recently enrolled are sent; the rest of the meeting’s voices are named the way they would be without enrollment. The bound exists so session setup stays quick, not as a limit on how many people may enroll.

If the lookup fails or is slow, the meeting starts with voice ids instead. A transcript matters more than a label, so nothing about this step can stop a meeting being transcribed.

Withdrawing consent

Section titled “Withdrawing consent”

The enrollee can withdraw at any time from the same portal, without asking anyone. Withdrawal is immediate and does three things: the centroid is deleted, the status becomes revoked, and a REVOKED row is appended to the log. No future meeting can match the voice, because there is no longer a voiceprint to match against.

Transcripts already produced keep the names they were given. Those are ordinary speaker labels on a stored transcript, not biometric data, and they are editable on the meeting page like any other name.

An admin can also revoke on somebody’s behalf — for a person who has left, say — which records the admin as the actor.

Retention

Section titled “Retention”

VoiceEnrollmentRetentionDays (default 365) bounds how long a consent stands without being renewed. A nightly pass deletes the centroid of any enrollment whose consent is older than that, sets it to revoked and appends an EXPIRED row. Consent that never expires is not meaningfully informed, and an organisation that wants a shorter cycle can set one.

Limits we hold deliberately

Section titled “Limits we hold deliberately”
  • Meeting audio is never enrolled. A voiceprint is only ever created from audio somebody recorded in the portal after consenting. The system does not build a voiceprint from a meeting, however much speech it has, and there is no setting that makes it do so.
  • Enrollment is never implied. Attending a meeting, being named in a transcript, or having an LMA account does not enroll anybody.
  • The sample audio is discarded, as above.
  • An admin cannot consent for somebody else. An admin can revoke; only the enrollee can grant, and there is no administrative action that creates a voiceprint.
  • No cross-deployment sharing. A centroid stays in the account and table it was created in.
  • People outside the deployment cannot enroll yet. Enrolling requires an account here, so the external participant and the contractor in a meeting room — the people this feature is most useful for — are not served by it today. Inviting them would mean issuing them credentials to this deployment, which is a larger decision than enrollment, and it is deliberately not part of this feature. Their voices are named the way they were before: by hand on the Speakers panel, or from what they say in the meeting.
  • A match is not yet distinguishable from a typed name. See how a match is used.

Deploying it

Section titled “Deploying it”
ParameterDefaultEffect
EnableVoiceEnrollmentfalseCreates the tables, the portal and the matching step, and adds Enroll My Voice to the navigation. While false, none of it exists.
VoiceEnrollmentRetentionDays365How long a consent stands before it must be renewed.
VoiceEnrollmentTermsVersion1Bump to require everybody to re-consent to new terms.

Both enrolling and matching need the on-demand ASR MicroVM engine (EnableMicrovmAsr=true), because that is the engine that computes speaker embeddings — one to build a voiceprint, the other to compare against it. Amazon Transcribe exposes none, so with enrollment on and that engine off the tables, the portal and the consent log all exist and work, and the step that would create a voiceprint reports that the engine is missing.
