Overview

Section titled “Overview”

Not sure which capture option to use? See Meeting Sources for a side-by-side comparison of the Chrome Extension, Stream Audio, and Virtual Participant.

The Virtual Participant (VP) is a headless Chrome browser that joins meetings as a separate participant, driven by Playwright. It runs on ECS (EC2 or Fargate) or, where available, in an AWS Lambda MicroVM — see Launch Types. It captures audio and metadata, sending them to the LMA Kinesis Data Stream for transcription and processing.

When to use Virtual Participant

Section titled “When to use Virtual Participant”
  • Attendees on native desktop or mobile apps: Participants are free to use native meeting apps (Zoom, Teams, etc.) instead of being limited to the browser — the Chrome Extension and Stream Audio require joining from the web client.
  • Independent attendance: The VP can join before you arrive, stay after you leave, or attend meetings you do not join at all — including scheduling it in advance.
  • Voice Assistant: The VP is the only capture option that supports the Voice Assistant (wake phrase, push-to-talk, always-on modes).
  • Open VP live view: Use the Meeting Assistant’s “Open VP live view” feature to see the bot’s browser view of the meeting in real time.

See Meeting Sources for the full comparison.

Several people behind one attendee

Section titled “Several people behind one attendee”

A Virtual Participant names speakers from the meeting roster, which is exact for an ordinary remote attendee and useless for a conference room: everybody sharing one speakerphone shares one roster entry, so a whole room arrives as a single speaker.

Virtual Participant voice separation, in the Transcription Engine section of Configuration ▸ Configuration, fixes that. With it on, the VP tells apart the distinct voices behind one attendee and labels them Conference Room (spk_0), Conference Room (spk_1) and so on — the roster name for who the platform says is talking, the voice id for which voice within it.

Name them on the Speakers panel, which lists one row per voice and relabels every segment that voice produced.

The voice ids are shown only where they tell people apart. Each roster name is shown as one person — Bob Strahan for every line, however many voice ids separation gave him — unless it reads as a room (Conference Room, ConfRm_1, a room device) or two of its voices each spoke at length, in which case it is shown as several people: Conference Room · Voice 1, Voice 2. The One person | Several people menu beside the name’s chips on the Transcript tab (and beside the name in the Speakers panel) corrects either guess, and in Several people each voice can be named in place; see One person or several people.

The other two naming routes do not apply to a Virtual Participant today, and it is worth knowing which: naming from what people say only considers voices whose base name is a placeholder such as Other Participant, and a VP’s base name is always a real roster name; and voiceprints are seeded only into streaming sessions, not into a VP’s.

It works on either transcription engine. On Amazon Transcribe the turns are recovered from Transcribe’s own per-item speaker labels; on the on-demand engine they come from that engine’s own separation. Either way the switch is read once at the start of each meeting, so it takes effect on the next meeting with no redeployment.

Three limits are worth knowing.

Labels arrive only when a segment finalises. Amazon Transcribe caps a result at about thirty seconds, so the live transcript can show one roster name, and no voice ids, for that long before the row splits into voices. The words themselves are not lost; a watcher simply sees the separation late.

Accuracy depends on the audio, and on which engine. A room heard through a speakerphone, compressed by the meeting platform and mixed with everyone else, is the hardest input separation gets.

Measured on a deployed stack with four synthesised voices mixed onto one channel, 33 seconds, identical audio to both engines:

EngineDistinct labels for 4 voicesTime-weighted purity
Amazon Transcribe257%
On-demand engine379%

Purity here is the share of labelled time whose label maps to a single real person; 100% would mean every label belongs to exactly one speaker. Prefer the on-demand engine for rooms. On Amazon Transcribe each label straddled more than one person, so naming those rows attaches a real name to a substantial share of somebody else’s words — which is worse than the unseparated Conference Room, because that at least claims nothing it cannot support.

Both engines merged the speaker whose turns were under about a second (“Agreed.”, “Fine by me.”): too little audio to characterise a voice. Expect short interjections to be attributed to whoever spoke around them.

Synthesised voices may be more alike than real ones and the test had no room reverb or background noise, so treat the figures as directional rather than a guarantee for your room.

To see how a particular room separated, set the stack’s LogLevel parameter to DEBUG. The Virtual Participant then logs one [DIARIZATION] line per Transcribe result, giving the number of segments it produced and the speaker it assigned to each — which is what tells you whether the labels or the platform’s active-speaker signal is the thing going wrong.

Note what that writes: the speaker names on those lines are participant names, so at DEBUG they reach CloudWatch Logs. No transcript text is written by this line. Turn the level back down once you have what you need.

Where the two signals disagree, the finer boundary wins: a voice label running across a change of platform speaker is split at the change, and the platform’s name is the one each part carries (see How Speaker Names Are Assigned). A turn split in two can be put right with Only this segment on the meeting page, which is the affordance for a single turn attributed to the wrong voice; a turn merged under one name cannot be separated at all. (Renaming on the Speakers panel would not help here — it is per voice, so it would rename every turn that voice produced, not the one that was misattributed.)

The voice assistant’s own speech is transcribed on a channel of its own on both engines, so it never acquires a voice id.

How Speaker Names Are Assigned

Section titled “How Speaker Names Are Assigned”

A participant row is named after the attendee the meeting platform showed as speaking during that row’s audio, not after whoever is showing when the row reaches the transcript, which is a second or more later. The Virtual Participant notes each change of active speaker on the transcription audio clock at the moment the platform reports it, so the change and the words are on the same clock.

  • On Amazon Transcribe each word goes to the attendee shown for most of that word, and a result is cut into separate rows wherever that changes. A partial (in-progress) row carries the attendee shown for most of it until its final arrives and is cut.
  • On the on-demand engine the engine has already cut the rows, and each row goes to the attendee shown for most of it.
  • The Virtual Participant’s own name is never used for a participant row, and when the platform shows nobody speaking the last attendee shown carries on.

The platform reports a change slightly after it happens, so each change is moved back by an allowance before rows are matched to it:

PlatformAllowanceWhy
Zoom, web client0 sThe page reports the change as it happens. On a three-person Zoom meeting it showed the Virtual Participant as speaking 0.08–0.36 s (median 0.16 s) after the Virtual Participant’s own audio started, and other attendees’ changes landed in the pause before their first transcribed word.
Zoom, Meeting SDK2 sThe SDK monitor polls every 3 seconds.
Teams, Chime, Webex, Google Meet0.75 sThe default for the other platforms.

The ASR_ROSTER_LAG_SECONDS environment variable on the Virtual Participant task overrides the allowance for every platform.

How the voice label combines with the name (Amazon Transcribe). With voice separation on, the name is always the platform’s, and Transcribe’s voice label is kept only as a hint. At any moment a label is shown with one attendee only: the attendee it has coincided with for the most speech so far in the Transcribe session. Under anyone else the row carries the bare name. So a room’s voices, all of which coincide mostly with the room, still read Conference Room (spk_0), Conference Room (spk_1). A remote attendee’s row that Transcribe happens to label with a voice it mostly hears from somebody else reads just Ana Ruiz, not Ana Ruiz (spk_3) alongside Sam Lee (spk_3).

The platform’s name wins because it is exact for a remote attendee, while Transcribe’s labels on the mixed meeting audio are not. On the same three-person Zoom meeting, one label coincided with all three attendees over the course of the meeting. Two consequences are worth knowing:

  • Voice labels restart with each Transcribe session, as Transcribe numbers voices afresh after a reconnect.
  • If a label’s majority moves to a different attendee during the meeting, rows written before the move keep what they were given. On that meeting, this happened to two of its five labels, once each.

The on-demand engine appends its voice id to the platform’s name as before.

Google Meet

Section titled “Google Meet”

The VP joins a Google Meet meeting as a guest from its browser, without a Google account, under its display name (LMA (<your user name>) by default). On the create form choose Google Meet and enter the meeting code (abc-defg-hij) or paste the meeting link (https://meet.google.com/abc-defg-hij); Meet meetings have no password, so the form does not ask for one, and a link is stored as its code. A meet.google.com/lookup/<name> link is not accepted: it uses a meeting nickname, which Google lets only signed-in members of the organiser’s Google Workspace use, and the VP joins as a guest, so enter the meeting’s code instead. Pasting a Meet calendar invitation into Paste Invitation fills the form in the same way. The lma vp create --platform GOOGLE_MEET command, the SDK, and the meeting assistant’s start_meeting_now and schedule_meeting tools accept Meet too.

On the pre-join screen the VP types its name under What’s your name?, turns the microphone off (unless a voice assistant needs it) and the camera off (unless the Simli avatar is its camera), and presses Ask to join, or Join now when Meet lets it straight in. It then waits, for up to five minutes, until it is in the meeting, posts its introduction in the meeting chat (In-call messages) and starts transcribing.

What the meeting’s organiser has to allow.

  • Guests from a browser. Meet has to accept a guest who is not signed in to a Google account. Google offers this for meetings organised from a Google Workspace account whose administrator allows participants from outside the organisation and without a Google account; a meeting organised from a personal Google account may not accept such guests at all. When Meet does not accept the VP, it shows “You can’t join this video call” before any name field. The VP then reloads the page, up to four times, after pauses of 4, 8, 15 and 20 seconds (about 50 seconds in all, plus the page loads), and clears the browser’s Google cookies before the second and later reloads. If Meet still refuses it, the VP stops with FAILED and the detail “Google Meet did not let the participant join” (endTrigger pre-join:meet-refused).
  • Being let in. Somebody in the meeting has to admit the VP when it asks to join, unless the meeting lets guests in without asking. With a Google Workspace organiser, setting the meeting’s Meeting access to Open is meant to let anyone with the link join without being admitted. In live attempts a guest with no Google account joined an Open meeting without being admitted. A request that is declined ends the join with pre-join:meet-denied, and one that nobody answers within five minutes with pre-join:not-admitted.
  • Chat. The chat commands (LMA leave, LMA pause and the rest) and the introduction need the meeting chat. If the organiser has turned chat off for participants, the VP posts nothing and cannot be dismissed from the chat; remove it from the meeting from Meet’s People panel instead, or end it from LMA.

What has been observed in live attempts, from a browser on an Amazon EC2 host and not signed in, to a meeting whose organiser had set Meeting access to Open: every join that reused the browser profile saved from an earlier Meet join was refused with “You can’t join this video call”, and the first join from a fresh profile got in. A page load that Meet refused has also been followed by a reload that showed the name field, which is why the VP reloads before giving up. So a Google Meet join always starts from a fresh browser profile, and the VP never saves its profile after a Meet meeting, unlike the per-user profile kept for the other platforms (see Zoom Sign-in). Meet’s reasons for refusing a guest are not documented, so these are observations rather than an explanation; treat Google Meet as working only where a test meeting shows it does.

What is not available, or not yet confirmed, on Google Meet.

  • Confirmed in a live meeting: joining, the introduction and the chat (including LMA ask and the assistant and translator switches), the voice assistant, and leaving when the host ends the meeting for everyone. The VP opens Meet in English (hl=en) whatever the browser’s language, and falls back to the AI resolver for the join controls and the chat button.
  • Speaker names are not confirmed yet. The VP reads the audio-level indicator on each participant tile to tell who is speaking. That reading is built from one live meeting and has not yet been confirmed while people speak, so transcript lines may still carry the voice-separation label (spk_0, spk_1) rather than participants’ names; name the voices in the Speakers panel. When no tile reads as speaking and exactly one other named participant is in the meeting, the VP names that participant as the speaker, so a two-person meeting is attributed either way. The VP logs each change in a tile’s speaking state ([meet] tile … speaking=…, the first 50 per meeting), the structure of the first tiles and, at debug level, which tile elements change while people speak ([meet] tile … activity) over the first 45 seconds of the meeting, or until it ends if that is sooner.
  • Meeting consent: Notify, both Opt-in rules and objections are offered. A consent typed in the chat is recorded under the sender the VP reads from the chat, as on Zoom and Chime, and one said aloud under the participant the VP shows speaking. Neither reading has been confirmed in a live Meet meeting yet (see the speaker names above); see Meeting Consent.
  • Screen-share key frames: Meet gives the VP no share signal it reads, so key frames are sampled all the time with the stricter thresholds, as on Webex and Chime.
  • The Simli avatar has not been tested on Meet. Joining with a Google account signed in to the VP’s browser profile is not supported; the VP joins as a guest.

Joining a Meeting

Section titled “Joining a Meeting”
  1. Navigate to Virtual Participant in the LMA UI.
  2. Enter meeting details: URL, platform, meeting ID/password, and meeting name.
  3. Click Join Now.
  4. The VP starts in approximately 20-25 seconds (MicroVM, the default), 30-60 seconds (EC2), or 1-2 minutes (Fargate).
  5. Once joined, the VP posts an introduction message in the meeting chat.
  6. View VP status in the UI as it progresses through its lifecycle (see Status Lifecycle).

Reviewing the launch settings

Section titled “Reviewing the launch settings”

The Virtual Participant page has a Launch settings section (collapsed by default) that lists what the Create Virtual Participant form asked for, in three groups:

  • Meeting: meeting name, platform, meeting ID, URL or Google Meet code, whether a meeting password was given (the password itself is never shown), the Zoom account choice for Zoom meetings, the meeting time (Start now, or the scheduled time in the time zone it was entered in and in yours when they differ), and the agenda.
  • Capture: whether the meeting video (screen) is recorded. Audio recording and the transcript are not a per-meeting choice.
  • Consent: the meeting consent choice for this meeting, or Not set (deployment default) when the deployment’s Meeting Consent setting applies.

A setting the form did not send, such as on a Virtual Participant started from an MCP client or created before the setting was recorded, reads Not set (deployment default) or Not recorded.

Status Lifecycle

Section titled “Status Lifecycle”

The VP reports a granular status as it boots, joins, and runs. The UI uses these to give a precise picture of where in the startup sequence the VP is:

StatusWhat’s happening
INITIALIZINGStep Functions has submitted the ECS RunTask request (or RunMicrovm under MICROVM); the VP is being scheduled
WAITING_FOR_CAPACITYThe task is queued waiting for compute placement (typically 60-90 seconds). On EC2, the capacity-provider auto-scaler may be launching a new host (see Auto-Scaling); on Fargate, this is the brief serverless provisioning / networking step. The UI tailors this message to your deployment’s VPLaunchType.
BOOTINGContainer started; pulling Chrome image, starting Xvfb / VNC / PulseAudio
REGISTERING_NETWORKRegistering the task with the live-view ALB (typically 30-60 seconds). Skipped under MICROVM, which has no ALB — each MicroVM exposes its own HTTPS endpoint.
HYDRATING_PROFILERestoring the per-user Chromium profile (cookies, “trusted device” markers) from S3
LAUNCHING_BROWSERLaunching Chromium via Playwright (chromium.launchPersistentContext)
VNC_READYBrowser is up; live-view viewer can connect
CONNECTINGInitializing audio/video pipelines (Nova Sonic, Simli avatar, agent mic)
JOININGNavigating to the meeting URL; signing in to Zoom if credentials are stored
MANUAL_ACTION_REQUIREDA CAPTCHA, 2FA, SSO, consent dialog, or other Zoom verification step needs human input — see Manual Action Required
ACTIVE / JOINEDVP is in the meeting and capturing audio
COMPLETED / ENDEDMeeting ended cleanly (host removed the VP, all attendees left, or the VP got “lonely”)

When ECS reports a “soft failure” on RunTask (returns HTTP 200 with a non-empty failures array — typically the container instance agent is briefly disconnected), the state machine catches it explicitly and writes FAILED plus the original failure reason to the VP record, instead of leaving the VP stuck in INITIALIZING indefinitely. That write is conditional on the row not having reached a terminal status, so a task that did start and is reporting its own outcome keeps it.

The status detail line

Section titled “The status detail line”

Alongside status, the VP record carries two fields describing the current status in more detail:

FieldContents
errorMessageThe prose the UI shows under the status. It is the detail line for every status, not only failures: the reason a meeting ended on COMPLETED (“Asked to leave by Jeremy.”), the current sub-step while the VP is still JOINING (“Waiting to be admitted…”), and the error on FAILED.
endTriggerThe machine-readable counterpart: which branch the VP exited through, from the vocabulary below. Written on COMPLETED and FAILED, so join outcomes across many meetings can be counted with a query over the VP table instead of a search through container logs.

Reaching JOINED, ACTIVE or COMPLETED without a new detail to show clears errorMessage, so a message from earlier in the same run — a join sub-step, or an attempt that did not work out before a later one did — is not still displayed next to a VP that is in the meeting.

The rest are a fixed prefix plus a detail that came from the platform, so the prefix is what groups and what follows the colon varies:

FormWritten whenDetail after the colon
ACS_REMOVED:…, ACS_DISCONNECTED:…The Teams SDK reported the call disconnected, or the VP removed from itThe SDK’s own code and subCode, or no-endreason
webex-text:…A Webex meeting-end phrase appeared on the pageWhich of five phrases matched

poll-error:…The Chime meeting-end poll threwThe start of the underlying browser message

Note that error: appears in both tables and means different things in each. The six values in the first are written by the container’s own classifier when a join throws, and are fixed. An error: value that is not one of those six came from the Webex watcher after admission, and its detail is whatever the browser said — so the value space is open, and this page cannot list it. Group those by prefix and read errorMessage for the detail, which is where prose belongs.

Whatever follows the first colon is reduced to a lower-case, hyphenated slug before the value is stored, and the whole value is capped at 100 characters. That is what keeps the second table countable: two reports of the same failure become the same string whatever the wording or punctuation around them. A trigger with no colon is stored exactly as it is.

COMPLETED means the VP was in the meeting. A VP that never got in — not admitted, or stuck on the pre-join screen — ends as FAILED with “Could not join the meeting (was not admitted, or stuck on the join screen).”, on every platform.

How the VP decides a meeting has ended

Section titled “How the VP decides a meeting has ended”

The VP leaves on its own so an abandoned meeting is not recorded (or billed) indefinitely. It uses several signals, in order of confidence:

  1. An in-meeting chat command — LMA leave and its synonyms; see In-meeting Chat Commands.
  2. A positive end-of-meeting signal from the platform — on Teams, the post-meeting screen (“You left the meeting”, “The meeting has ended”) or a post-meeting URL, checked before anything else on every poll, so a meeting Teams announces as over is detected on the first poll. The hang-up button disappearing is a second such signal, watched continuously rather than on the poll cycle.
  3. A sustained empty roster — the participant count is present and reads one or fewer for several consecutive polls.
  4. A sustained absence of the whole meeting UI — neither a readable participant count nor any in-meeting controls. This is the backstop for the case where none of the above fires.

On Google Meet the same four signals are read from Meet’s page. The end-of-meeting signal is Meet’s own screen once its Leave call button has gone (“You’ve been removed from the meeting”, “The call has ended”, “You left the meeting”), or the page leaving meet.google.com; the screen’s wording is only read once the button has gone, so chat text that happens to contain it is not mistaken for the end of the meeting. The participant count is the number on the People button, and an unreadable count with the Leave call button still on screen does not count towards leaving, as with Teams below. Meet uses the default bounds below; the three Teams settings do not apply to it.

The distinction between the third and fourth signals matters, and getting it wrong is what caused #660. Teams removes the roster button, and with it the participant-count badge, whenever the meeting toolbar collapses — most commonly during a content share or in full-screen layouts. An unreadable badge therefore says nothing on its own: it is equally the signature of a meeting that ended and of a meeting where someone is presenting. So before an unreadable count counts towards leaving at all, the VP checks whether the rest of the in-meeting interface (the stage, the calling screen, the call controls) is still present, and while it is, the VP stays. That matters because nothing in the VP moves the pointer, so a toolbar that auto-hides during a share can stay hidden for the whole presentation.

That check is itself bounded, deliberately, because Teams is not reliable in the other direction either: it sometimes leaves in-meeting controls on screen after a meeting has ended, which is how a Virtual Participant once kept recording long after everyone had gone. So the in-meeting interface can hold the VP in a meeting with an unreadable roster for about 30 consecutive minutes, after which the unreadable count starts counting again and the VP leaves about five minutes later. A readable count at any point restores the full allowance, so a meeting containing several long shares is fine; it is one unbroken half-hour with no readable roster at all that is treated as suspicious. The worst case in either direction is therefore bounded: a share is safe for half an hour at a stretch, and a meeting whose end Teams announces to nobody is left after about 35 minutes at the default cadence — and never more than an hour, whatever the settings below.

Three settings control the timing, in the Virtual Participant section of the Configuration page, where they can be changed while the stack runs, with no stack update. They apply to the Teams browser join path only — Zoom has its own equivalent, and the Teams ACS SDK join path (used when AcsConnectionString is configured) gets deterministic roster events instead and needs none of this. Both the ECS and MicroVM launch types honour them. The defaults suit Teams as it behaves today; change them only if a deployment is consistently leaving meetings early, or lingering after they end:

SettingDefaultMeaning
Teams: milliseconds between meeting checks20000How often the VP samples the meeting UI, in milliseconds. Range 1000–120000.
Teams: checks alone before leaving3Consecutive polls reading one or fewer attendees before the VP leaves — about 60 seconds at the default cadence. Range 1–90.
Teams: checks without a meeting before leaving15Consecutive polls with neither a readable attendee count nor any in-meeting controls before the VP leaves — about 5 minutes at the default cadence. Range 1–90.

A value saved on the Configuration page takes effect on the next Virtual Participant to start. Editing the VP task definition by hand does not work: both launch types reference a specific task-definition revision, so a revision registered outside CloudFormation is never used. To change one, an Admin makes and activates a Custom configuration profile (see Configuration profiles) and saves the setting there.

Inside the container the three values are the VP_ATTENDEE_POLL_MS, VP_POLLS_BEFORE_END and VP_POLLS_BEFORE_END_MISSING environment variables, and a value saved on the page replaces the variable when the Virtual Participant starts; the variables are also how they can be set when running the container locally. The container also bounds them on its own, in both directions: a value that is not a whole number in range is ignored in favour of the built-in default, and each tolerance is held inside a wall-clock window however the cadence and the count combine: 4 to 30 minutes for an unreadable meeting, and 30 seconds to 10 minutes for an empty one. So lowering the cadence for more frequent logging cannot accidentally narrow a tolerance to seconds, and raising a count cannot leave a VP running without a backstop. A count outside its window is adjusted rather than honoured, with a line in the log saying so — at the default cadence, for instance, Teams: checks without a meeting before leaving below 12 is raised, despite the page accepting 1.

Every decision the watchdog makes is logged. The resolved values appear once per meeting next to Listening for attendee changes; each poll logs its running counter against the bound that applies to it; and a poll held back by the in-meeting interface reports which element it matched, along with how much of the 30-minute allowance is left. If a Virtual Participant ever leaves a meeting that was still running, or lingers after one ended, those lines are what to look at first.

Manual Action Required (CAPTCHA, 2FA, SSO)

Section titled “Manual Action Required (CAPTCHA, 2FA, SSO)”

When the VP can’t proceed without a human (Zoom 2FA passcode, CAPTCHA, SSO redirect, an unknown consent dialog the auto-dismiss handler can’t classify), it sets status to MANUAL_ACTION_REQUIRED and surfaces:

  • A persistent Flashbar alert at the top of the LMA UI with the action type and a link to the meeting detail page.
  • An action banner on the Virtual Participant detail page above the live VNC viewer.
  • A browser notification + audio chime if you’ve granted notification permission, so you can be tabbed-away or in another window.

Open the live VNC viewer for the affected VP, complete the challenge (type the passcode, solve the CAPTCHA, sign in via SSO, click the consent button — whatever the banner says), and the VP picks up automatically. The default timeout is 3 minutes; if no human response arrives, the VP fails the meeting cleanly.

The banner is dismissible — dismissed alerts are remembered in localStorage (per VP id) so the banner doesn’t reappear on every page refresh after you’ve dealt with it.

AI-driven self-healing

Section titled “AI-driven self-healing”

Every platform handler (Zoom, Teams, Webex, Chime, Google Meet) wraps its hard-coded CSS selectors in an AI fallback resolver. When a primary selector misses (because the meeting platform shipped a UI change), the resolver asks Claude (Bedrock, vision-capable) to find the right element by reading a compact DOM summary plus a screenshot of the current page. Successful resolutions are cached in a shared DomSelectorCache DynamoDB table (30-day TTL on lastUsedAt) so the cost is paid only once per platform UI change.

The same resolver classifies unknown popup dialogs:

  • CONSENT / RECORDING_NOTICE → auto-clicked by the VP.
  • CAPTCHA / LOGIN_REQUIRED / SSO_REDIRECT / BLOCKED → escalated to MANUAL_ACTION_REQUIRED (see above) so a human can solve it via the live VNC viewer.

Disable the AI fallback by setting BedrockDomResolverModelId to an empty string at deploy time — the VP reverts to hardcoded-selector-only behavior.

For full details on the AI resolver and its interaction with the Zoom sign-in flow, see Zoom Sign-in & Join Reliability.

Zoom Sign-in

Section titled “Zoom Sign-in”

LMA can sign the VP in to Zoom using per-user stored credentials before navigating to the meeting URL. A signed-in session joins far more reliably (“We detected you may be a bot…” guest blocks become rare) and allows the VP to join meetings that disallow guests.

  • Each user adds their own credentials via the Zoom account card in the Create Virtual Participant modal. Credentials live in AWS Secrets Manager keyed by Cognito sub, and the plaintext password is never returned to the React UI.
  • The VP container reads the secret at runtime; it is never put on the task definition or in the Step Functions execution input.
  • The sign-in flow is AI-driven end-to-end. After submitting the username, Claude inspects each subsequent page (password entry, OTP, passkey-binding upsell, phone-binding upsell, dashboard, etc.) and decides whether to fill, skip, click-through, wait, or escalate — so it tolerates Zoom’s frequent post-login interstitials without code changes.
  • 2FA / CAPTCHA / SSO challenges that need human input fall through to MANUAL_ACTION_REQUIRED (see above).
  • Per-user persistent Chromium profile in S3 means cookies and “trusted device” markers survive across meetings — after the first manual sign-in, subsequent VPs reuse the session and sign in cleanly without re-prompting.

For setup, caveats, and the full join-reliability stack, see Zoom Sign-in & Join Reliability.

Changing a scheduled Virtual Participant

Section titled “Changing a scheduled Virtual Participant”

While a Virtual Participant is waiting for its scheduled launch, its owner and Admins see an Edit button on the Launch settings section of its page. It opens the Create Virtual Participant form’s fields, filled in with the stored values: the meeting name, platform, meeting ID or link, meeting time, agenda, meeting consent, video recording and, for Zoom, the stored Zoom account choice. The meeting password field starts empty, and leaving it empty keeps the current password. The stored Zoom account used is always that of the user who scheduled the meeting, whoever makes the change.

Changes are accepted until 5 minutes before the meeting time, and a new meeting time must also be at least 5 minutes away. The check is made again when the change is saved: if the Virtual Participant has started launching in the meantime, the change is refused and it launches as it was scheduled.

A saved change moves the launch schedule: the scheduler rewrites the one EventBridge Scheduler schedule created for the Virtual Participant, so it launches once, at the new time and with the new settings. If the schedule cannot be moved (for example because it no longer exists), no new schedule is created, and the Virtual Participant’s status line says that it will launch as it was scheduled before; cancel it and schedule a new one to use the new settings. The agenda and meeting consent choice are read by the Virtual Participant when it starts, so changes to them apply even without moving the schedule.

How a viewer is authorized

Section titled “How a viewer is authorized”

Opening the viewer takes two steps, both of which happen automatically in the UI, and rests on a third control at the load balancer.

1. A token for the connection. Before it opens the WebSocket, the viewer calls a GraphQL mutation to mint a token for the specific Virtual Participant it is about to watch. The resolver looks the VP up in DynamoDB and mints only for the VP’s owner, a user it has been shared with, or a member of the Admin group. Which mutation it calls depends on the launch type:

Launch typeMutationTransport
MICROVMcreateMicrovmVncToken(vpId)Port-scoped auth token passed as a WebSocket subprotocol to the MicroVM’s own endpoint
EC2 / FARGATEcreateVncEdgeToken(vpId)Token passed as the token query parameter on wss://<cloudfront>/vnc/<vpId>

For the ECS launch types the token is base64url(payload).base64url(HMAC-SHA256(payload)). The payload names the vpId, the /vnc/<vpId> path, the target port, and an expiry a few minutes out. A Lambda@Edge viewer-request function on the CloudFront /vnc/* behavior recomputes the MAC and forwards the request only when the signature matches, the expiry is inside the permitted window, and both the vpId and the path in the payload equal the ones in the request. The signing key is generated per deployment into AWS Secrets Manager and read only by the minting resolver and the edge function; the edge function fetches it once per cold start from the stack’s home region (Lambda@Edge takes no environment variables and supports no layers, so the secret ARN and region are substituted into its source at deploy time) and refuses the request if it cannot read it. Both mutations mint on demand per viewer session, so the viewer’s existing auto-reconnect simply mints again.

This is one of three controls, not the whole of it. The edge function sees traffic that arrives through this deployment’s CloudFront distribution, which is what step 3 below establishes for every route into a task; the credential in step 2 applies at the VNC server itself.

2. A credential for the VNC server. On the ECS launch types the VP generates a random VNC credential when it boots and publishes it on its own record; the createVncEdgeToken response carries it back to the already-authorized viewer, which supplies it to the server. The credential belongs to one task and is replaced on every boot. Under MICROVM the VP runs as a pre-snapshot stack at image-build time, where there is no per-meeting record to publish a credential to and any credential would be captured in the snapshot and shared by every launch; there the framebuffer is bound to the VM’s loopback interface and the port-scoped auth token is the only route in.

3. An origin-verify header on the load balancer. The CloudFront distribution attaches a per-deployment header value to every request it sends to the VNC load balancer, and the load balancer forwards only requests carrying it — anything else gets a 403. This applies to both routes into a task: the rule the AI stack creates for the shared target group, and the rule each Virtual Participant creates for its own path at run time. The participant is given the secret’s ARN, not its value, and reads the value from Secrets Manager once when it starts, so the value never appears in its task definition; if it cannot read the value it does not create a routing rule at all, and the participant fails to start rather than being reachable without the header.

The load balancer’s security group additionally admits only CloudFront’s com.amazonaws.global.cloudfront.origin-facing managed prefix list. That is a useful coarse filter, and it establishes that a request came from CloudFront. The header establishes the narrower property: that it came through this deployment’s own distribution.

If you are upgrading, these take effect only after the Virtual Participant container image is rebuilt — both are applied in code that runs inside the container:

  • The framebuffer’s loopback binding (entrypoint.sh). Under MICROVM this runs at image-build time, so it lands when the MicroVM snapshot is rebuilt; existing snapshots keep the previous binding until then.
  • The origin-verify condition on the per-participant routing rule (status-manager.ts).

Everything else in this section — the token check at the edge, the mutations, and the load balancer’s own rule and default response — is CloudFormation and applies as soon as the stack update completes.

Virtual Participants that are already running when the stack updates keep the routing rule their task created; those rules are deleted when each participant’s meeting ends, by the same cleanup that removes its target group, so they clear on their own as those meetings finish. A rule can outlive its task if that task ended without the cleanup running; a participant started afterwards on the rebuilt image replaces any rule it finds on its own path before creating its own, and a leftover rule for a participant that no longer exists can be deleted from the load balancer’s listener by hand (its VirtualParticipantId tag names the participant it belonged to).

Known limitations

Section titled “Known limitations”

These are current trade-offs rather than oversights, recorded so they are visible when planning changes to this path.

The ECS token is a bearer credential in a URL. Browsers cannot set headers on a WebSocket handshake, so the token travels as the token query parameter, and the /vnc/* behavior sets ForwardedValues: QueryString: true. It therefore appears in CloudFront access logs and in browser history, and it is a bearer credential — valid for whoever presents it, with nothing binding it to the browser it was minted for. Its few-minute lifetime and the VNC server credential are what bound that. The MicroVM path avoids carrying the token in the URL at all, by passing it as a WebSocket subprotocol, which is the same trick applied to a different endpoint. The same approach could in principle be used here, but websockify would have to be configured to accept and ignore the extra subprotocol, and the edge function would have to read it from the Sec-WebSocket-Protocol header instead of the query string; that has not been done.

The edge function calls Secrets Manager cross-region on a cold start. A viewer-request trigger is capped at 5 seconds and 128 MB, and the signing key lives in one region while the function runs replicated in whichever edge region is nearest the viewer. The call is made once per container and cached for its lifetime, and the function refuses the request if it cannot complete — so the failure mode is a viewer in a distant region intermittently being unable to open the live view, not an unchecked request. Lambda@Edge cannot be pre-warmed. Worth watching after a first deployment.

Rotating the signing key requires a deliberate rollover. The key is cached for the life of each edge container with no TTL, and nothing rotates it on a schedule. Replacing the secret’s value therefore leaves already-warm replicas verifying against the previous key and rejecting newly minted tokens, for as long as those containers live — an interval that is not bounded by anything in the design. A rotation needs either a tolerated window of failures or a change that lets the function hold more than one key.

Rotating the origin-verify value is harder still, and needs a stack update. Five places hold it: the CloudFront origin’s header and the load balancer’s own rule, both resolved at stack create/update; the in-memory copy each running task read when it started; the literal baked into each per-participant rule already on the listener; and Secrets Manager itself, which newly started tasks read live. Replacing the secret’s value without a stack update leaves running participants working while every newly started one becomes unviewable — its rule carries the new value, the distribution still sends the old one, so requests fall through to the load balancer’s own rule and return a 503 from the shared target group, which holds no targets. Doing it with a stack update inverts that: new participants work and every already-running one becomes unviewable for the remainder of its meeting. Either way a rotation requires a stack update and interrupts live viewing for an interval bounded only by how long the longest running meeting lasts. There is no rotation schedule on this secret, or on any secret in this solution. Live viewing is a diagnostic aid rather than part of the capture path, so a rotation does not affect transcription or recording.

The hop from CloudFront to the load balancer is not encrypted. The vnc-alb origin is configured OriginProtocolPolicy: http-only, so the origin-verify header, the access token in the query string, the VNC server credential in the RFB handshake and the framebuffer itself all travel in clear text between the CloudFront edge and an internet-facing load balancer. HTTPS to this origin is not available: CloudFront requires a publicly trusted certificate matching the origin’s domain name, and one cannot be issued for an *.elb.amazonaws.com name. Moving the load balancer behind a CloudFront VPC origin is the change that removes this along with the public DNS name, and is the recommended direction for this path.

Opening the preview from the meeting assistant, and closing a failed session

The meeting assistant can open or close the VNC preview on a meeting page; it does so through the toggleVNCPreview mutation, which the meeting page receives through the onVNCPreviewToggle subscription. Launch Types

Section titled “Launch Types”

MicroVM launch type (default)

Section titled “MicroVM launch type (default)”

VPLaunchType=MICROVM is the default. It runs each Virtual Participant inside an AWS Lambda MicroVM — a Firecracker VM resumed from a memory+disk snapshot — instead of an ECS task. It runs the same container image as the ECS launch types; only the surrounding infrastructure differs.

Requirements and limits (all imposed by the service, not by LMA):

ArchitectureARM64 only
Regionsus-east-1, us-east-2, us-west-2, ap-northeast-1, eu-west-1
Max meeting duration8 hours (hard ceiling)
Connections per MicroVM8 (not adjustable)

What it changes:

  • No ALB. Each MicroVM returns its own HTTPS endpoint, so the VNC ALB, its target group, listener, security groups, CloudFront /vnc/* behavior and the Lambda@Edge auth function are not created at all. The viewer authenticates with a short-lived, port-scoped MicroVM auth token instead. This is why MICROVM also removes those resources from the AI stack.
  • No hosts to scale. No EC2 ASG, no capacity provider, no SSM host patching. Billing is per meeting rather than per warm instance, and there is no WAITING_FOR_CAPACITY wait for a host to launch.
  • Faster startup. The Firecracker snapshot is taken with Xvfb, x11vnc, websockify and PulseAudio already running, and the image-build /validate hook exercises a real Chromium so Lambda prefetches its snapshot pages. Measured on a live deployment: VNC ready in ~20-25s.
  • Config delivery. ECS passes per-meeting values as ContainerOverrides environment variables. MicroVM image environment variables are baked into the image and shared by every MicroVM launched from it (and capped at 50), so the launcher stages the full configuration in the VP task registry and the /run hook payload carries only the VP id. The service caps runHookPayload at 4096 bytes, which three Cognito JWTs alone exceed.
  • Egress through your VPC. A AWS::Lambda::NetworkConnector routes MicroVM outbound traffic through the private subnets, so the VP reaches meeting platforms from the same NAT gateway Elastic IP as an ECS task. This costs no hourly charge (NAT data processing applies, which the ECS launch types already pay) and keeps meeting-platform-visible behavior identical across launch types.

Still requires a VPC. The WebSocket transcriber mandates the VPC and its NAT gateways regardless of how the VP is hosted, so MICROVM does not remove the VPC stack — it removes the VP’s ALB.

EC2

Section titled “EC2”

EC2 launch type uses warm instances with cached Docker images. This provides 85-90% faster startup compared to cold Fargate launches, with the VP ready in approximately 30-60 seconds. The estimated cost is approximately $33/month for always-on instances.

EC2 is the recommended choice in regions where Lambda MicroVMs are not available and startup latency matters.

Fargate

Section titled “Fargate”

Fargate launch type is serverless and uses SOCI (Seekable OCI) for faster container image pulls, providing 40-60% faster startup than standard Fargate. The base cost is approximately $2/month, making it more economical for infrequent use. However, startup time is longer at 1-2 minutes.

EC2 Instance Types

Section titled “EC2 Instance Types”

Applies to VPLaunchType=EC2 only. Fargate and MicroVM have no instances to size.

Each VP container is capped at 3500 MB (observed peak memory is ~1650 MB with Chromium + Simli + Nova Sonic; the cap leaves headroom). A full voice-assistant + Simli-avatar meeting draws ~1.35 vCPU steady-state and runs reliably on the default t3.medium (the CloakBrowser stack downscales the avatar to 256×256@15fps and disables the emulated GPU, which keeps the encode cheap). Pick a larger instance mainly for more concurrent VPs per host rather than for a single meeting’s headroom — and ensure host memory accommodates your expected concurrent VPs plus ~600 MB for the OS / ECS agent:

General Purpose:

  • t3.medium (default) — 3867 MB host → 1 concurrent VP. The capacity-provider auto-scaler launches additional hosts when concurrent demand exceeds capacity, so users running one meeting at a time pay the baseline (~$30/month per host) and only scale up while multiple VPs are active.
  • t3.large — 7857 MB host → 3 concurrent VPs. Pick this if you regularly run 2-3 concurrent meetings and want fewer scale-outs.
  • t3.xlarge — 15.7 GB host → 6 concurrent VPs.

Compute-Optimized (non-burstable; for higher concurrency or guaranteed sustained CPU):

  • c5.large (2 vCPU) — a dedicated-CPU alternative to t3.medium for a single voice + avatar VP (no burstable credit limits)
  • c5.xlarge (4 vCPU) — multiple concurrent voice + avatar VPs per host
  • c5.2xlarge — heavier concurrency

Memory-Optimized (for large meeting context):

  • m5.large / m5.xlarge — when meeting context / model memory needs are higher

t3 instances are burstable: a single voice + avatar VP fits the t3.medium baseline, but if you run multiple concurrent VPs per host (or very long meetings), prefer c5.* / m5.* for guaranteed sustained CPU.

Auto-Scaling

Section titled “Auto-Scaling”

Applies to VPLaunchType=EC2 only. Fargate scales per task, and MicroVM launches one Firecracker VM per meeting with no cluster to scale.

The VP cluster uses an ECS capacity provider with managed scaling:

  • VPMinInstances (default 1) — Minimum warm hosts always running. Set to 0 to fully scale down when idle and pay only when a VP is requested (cold-start adds ~60-90s to the first VP).
  • VPMaxInstances (default 10) — Hard ceiling on concurrent hosts. With the t3.large default each host fits 3 VPs, so 10 hosts × 3 VPs = 30 concurrent meetings.

When concurrent demand exceeds the current cluster’s capacity, ECS automatically launches new hosts (TargetCapacity=100, step size 1-2, instance warmup 90s). The launching VP shows status WAITING_FOR_CAPACITY while the auto-scaler provisions a new host — typically 60-90 seconds — then transitions to BOOTING. Scale-down to VPMinInstances happens automatically when reservation drops.

ManagedTerminationProtection is enabled, so a host running an active VP will never be killed by scale-in until the VP exits.

Meeting Video Recording

Section titled “Meeting Video Recording”

In addition to the audio recording, the Virtual Participant can capture the meeting screen (screen shares, slides, participant video, the platform UI) as an MP4. The VP records its own X11 display — the same browser view you see in the live VNC viewer — with FFmpeg, uploads 60-second segments to S3 as the meeting runs (so a container crash loses at most the last segment), and assembles a single seekable .mp4 when the meeting ends.

Enabling / disabling

  • Per meeting: the Create Virtual Participant modal has a Record meeting video toggle (default on). Turn it off to capture audio + transcript only for that meeting.
  • Per deployment: the EnableVideoRecording CloudFormation parameter (default true) sets the default the toggle starts from and applies to launches that don’t specify a value (e.g. the start_meeting_now MCP tool).

Playback: once the meeting ends and the video finishes uploading, a Recording Video player appears in the meeting detail view alongside the Recording Audio player. The URL is stored on the call record (Call.VideoRecordingUrl) and presigned with your Cognito credentials at playback time — the same mechanism as the audio recording.

Storage & retention: final videos are written to the recordings bucket under lma-video-recordings/ and follow the same retention as audio (AudioRecordingExpirationInDays). In-progress segments under lma-video-chunks/ are deleted as soon as the final .mp4 is assembled; a 1-day S3 lifecycle rule reaps any chunks orphaned by a crash. Deleting the meeting deletes its video recording and any of its segments still in the bucket, as it does the audio recording. Expect roughly 30-50 MB per meeting at the default 5 fps (vs. ~5 MB for audio-only).

Resource cost: capture runs at 5 fps with libx264 -preset ultrafast to keep CPU overhead low (~5-10%). This fits comfortably on the default t3.medium host for a typical VP; if you run many concurrent video-recording VPs per host, size the instance up. Tunable via the VIDEO_FRAMERATE, VIDEO_RESOLUTION, and VIDEO_SEGMENT_DURATION task-definition environment variables.

Transcription Engine

Section titled “Transcription Engine”

A Virtual Participant is transcribed by Amazon Transcribe. With a voice assistant configured, the VP sends Amazon Transcribe one stream with two channels, with channel identification on: the meeting’s participants on the first channel and the assistant’s voice on the second, the same layout as Stream Audio’s tab and microphone. Participant rows are on the REMOTE channel, named from the meeting roster, and only they are checked for wake phrases and stop requests; the assistant’s rows are on the LOCAL channel under the VP’s own name. A participant who talks over the assistant therefore gets a line of their own, and the assistant can never wake or stop itself. A two-channel stream is billed for its duration, the same as one mono stream. Without a voice assistant the VP sends one mono stream of the meeting audio. The S3 audio recording is always the mix of both, as is the video recording’s audio track.

Virtual Participant voice separation, in the Transcription Engine section of Configuration ▸ Configuration (admin only), is on every deployment. It asks for per-voice labels, on either engine (on the on-demand engine, where its model bundle has speaker identification), so several people behind one attendee tile (a conference room, a shared screen playing a recording) come out as Name (spk_0), Name (spk_1). Off by default: speaker names come from the meeting roster, which is the better label for a normal attendee. See Several people behind one attendee.

A deployment with EnableMicrovmAsr=true can also move Virtual Participants onto the on-demand MicroVM ASR engine instead, from the same section, which then also shows the engine choices:

  • Virtual Participants: engine chooses the engine for every VP that starts after the change. The VP acquires and releases its own MicroVM, and falls back to Amazon Transcribe when none can be acquired.
  • The voice assistant is its own channel. With the assistant enabled, its speech is transcribed from a second engine session on the same MicroVM and appears on the LOCAL channel under the VP’s own name, never diarized, exactly as Stream Audio separates the microphone from the tab. Meeting rows are named after the attendee the platform showed during them (see How Speaker Names Are Assigned).
  • Pausing keeps the engine session. The chat pause command stops feeding meeting audio but leaves both sessions up, so the resume command continues the same transcript. If the engine dies mid-meeting, Amazon Transcribe takes over for the rest of it.

See MicroVM ASR for the engine itself.

  • When the chat platform exposes the sender name (Zoom, Chime, Webex, Google Meet), the goodbye message acknowledges who asked the VP to leave: “Thanks Alice — I’ll head out now.”
  • The default introduction message tells participants to type LMA leave to dismiss the bot and LMA help to see the other commands, and the name to add when there is more than one LMA in the meeting (see More than one LMA in a meeting). Customize via the IntroMessage CloudFormation parameter of the Virtual Participant stack or the INTRO_MESSAGE env var; a customized message is used as it is. In it, {LMA_USER} is replaced by the name of the user who started the VP, and {LMA_HANDLE} by the name to add after LMA to address this VP.
  • LMA pause, LMA start and LMA help must be the whole chat message: the LMA addressee (or @LMA, before or after the command) plus the command word, matched case-insensitively with punctuation ignored, so “@LMA, pause!” and “start LMA” count as the command. A bare PAUSE or START without the addressee is ordinary chat, as is a message that merely contains the command — “LMA PAUSED”, “please LMA pause now”, or an introduction message that explains the commands.
  • The help reply is posted as a few single-line chat messages, listing each command in one canonical form and how to address this VP by name. It is answered whether or not transcription is paused.
  • On the Zoom browser join the VP reads the chat from Zoom’s chat panel, which Zoom closes by itself, for one when someone starts sharing their screen. The VP reopens the panel within a few seconds whenever Zoom’s toolbar shows it closed, and then reads the messages sent while it was closed; a command typed while the panel was closed is therefore acted on a few seconds late rather than missed. It reads the messages already in the panel when it starts listening as sent before it joined and does not act on them, which matters on a meeting whose chat continues in a Team Chat group chat (Meeting Group Chat), where the panel can hold messages from earlier occurrences of the meeting.

More than one LMA in a meeting

Section titled “More than one LMA in a meeting”

When two or more people each invite their LMA Virtual Participant to the same meeting, LMA leave is addressed to all of them. So each VP acts on a chat command, a question, or a consent reply addressed to LMA only when it is addressed to that VP by name, or when it is the only LMA VP it knows to be in the meeting:

  • By name. A VP’s display name is the deployment’s LMA identity with its user filled in, LMA (<user>) by default. Typing or mentioning that name addresses the VP: “@LMA (Bob Strahan) leave”, “LMA (strahanr@example.com) summarize the meeting”. So does LMA followed by the part in parentheses, or its first word (the user’s first name, or the part of an email address before the @): “LMA Bob leave”, “@LMA strahanr help”, “LMA (Bob) what was decided?”. The rest of the message is then read as if it had been typed after a bare LMA, so every command, LMA ask, the bare question form and LMA consent work the same way. A first word that is itself a command or a question word (a user called “Help Desk”) is not used as a short name; the name in parentheses still works. When several VPs could match, the longest name wins, so with VPs for Bob Strahan and Bob Smith in one meeting “LMA Bob Smith leave” reaches only Bob Smith’s.
  • A short name two VPs share. With those two VPs, “LMA Bob leave” and “LMA (Bob) leave” could mean either, so neither acts on them. Each VP’s introduction and help reply then give its whole name in parentheses instead, “LMA (Bob Strahan) leave”, which reaches only that VP. Two VPs with the same display name cannot be told apart at all.
  • The bare LMA. LMA leave, @LMA help and the like are acted on as before when the VP knows of no other LMA VP in the meeting. When it knows of one or more, it does nothing with them — it does not post the help either — since it cannot tell which VP was meant.
  • Another VP’s name. A message addressed to another VP by its name, its short name, or any name in parentheses after LMA that is not this VP’s is left to that VP.
  • Not addressed to LMA. A consent reply that does not name LMA (“I agree”) is read by every VP, as before, and ordinary chat is unaffected.

The introduction message and the help reply say which name to add. A VP knows another one is in the meeting from a display name whose first word is LMA: a chat sender (every VP posts its introduction when it joins), a name the platform shows as speaking, a Google Meet participant tile, or the call’s participant list on the Teams Azure Communication Services join, which also shows when one has left. On the other joins, a VP is taken to have left when it posts its exit message (this deployment’s, or the default “Live Meeting Assistant has left the room.”, or the “Thanks — I’ll head out now.” it posts to whoever asked it to leave), or when it has not been seen for 10 minutes. Each chat message it posts, each time the platform shows it as speaking, and on Google Meet its tile (read every second) count as seeing it. So a VP that was removed from the meeting or stopped without posting its exit message stops counting after at most 10 minutes, and the bare LMA is answered again. The cost is on the Zoom and Teams browser joins, where a VP with no voice assistant that stays quiet in the chat is seen only once, when it posts its introduction: 10 minutes later it is no longer counted, and both VPs answer a bare LMA again, as they would without this feature. Address VPs by name to avoid that. A VP that has not yet appeared in any of these ways is not counted, so a VP that joins after another may answer a bare LMA until it has seen the first one; address VPs by name when there are several. A VP whose display name does not start with LMA (a customized identity) cannot be told from a person by its name. Messages from another LMA VP are never acted on. On Microsoft Teams a mention shows only the mentioned name, without the @; both forms work, as does Zoom’s @ mention.

Asking the Meeting Assistant from Chat

Section titled “Asking the Meeting Assistant from Chat”

LMA ask <question> (also @LMA ask <question>, LMA, ask: <question>) sends the question to the deployment’s Strands meeting assistant — the same agent the LMA meeting page’s assistant and the voice assistant’s lookups use — with the meeting’s transcript as context, and posts the answer in the meeting chat. @LMA <question> works too when the text after @LMA is not one of the other commands, so @LMA help is still help and @LMA translate still changes mode. A message that starts with LMA and is not one of the other commands is a question too when it reads as one: it ends with a question mark, or its first word is one of what, whats (what’s), who, whos (who’s), whom, whose, when, where, why, how, which, summarize, summarise, recap, explain, list or tell. That is the whole list: a message starting with any other word needs the question mark (or LMA ask). So “LMA what was just discussed?” is answered like “LMA ask what was just discussed?”, while “LMA is great” is ordinary chat and “LMA help?” is still help.

  • Availability. It needs a Strands meeting assistant (MeetingAssistService set to one of the STRANDS_BEDROCK options); it does not need a voice assistant. LMA help lists LMA ask only when one is configured. Without one, an explicit LMA ask is answered with a note saying so, and @LMA and LMA <question>? messages are ignored. For operators: on a Strands deployment any participant, including a guest, can ask, and each question is a meeting-assistant (Bedrock) request billed like any other. There is no separate switch to turn chat questions off; open an issue if your deployment needs one.
  • What the agent can use. A chat sender is known to the VP only by the display name the meeting shows, so a chat question is treated as any participant’s question. Because the answer is read by everyone in the meeting, it is answered from the current meeting’s transcript, its agenda and web search (where configured) only. Anyone in the meeting, including a guest, can therefore ask about the agenda of the meeting they are in (LMA ask what's left on the agenda?). The document knowledge base, the owner’s past meetings, MCP servers, and the VNC and browser-control tools are not offered for chat questions; ask through the LMA meeting page for answers that use them. For operators: an answer can draw on anything in the meeting’s transcript so far — including what was said before the person asking joined — and is posted where everyone in the meeting can read it.
  • How answers are posted. The answer is reduced to plain text on a single line, starting Live Meeting Assistant:. Markdown is removed; a link is shown as its text followed by its full address in parentheses, and a bare address is left whole, so it can be followed from the chat. Long answers are split at sentence ends into at most three messages, numbered (1/3), (2/3), …, and the rest is cut with an ellipsis. LMA keeps each message to 1,000 characters on Zoom and Teams, 500 on Webex and Chime, which post by typing, and 500 on Google Meet; these are LMA’s own conservative limits, not the platforms’ maximums.
  • Limits. Each participant may ask once every 20 seconds (told once if they ask sooner), questions over 500 characters are declined, and one question is answered at a time. On the Teams web client the sender is not visible, so everyone there shares one 20-second limit. Answers usually take a few seconds and can take longer when the agent searches; other commands are not held up meanwhile. If no answer arrives within 90 seconds the VP says so and takes the next question. While LMA pause is in effect questions are not sent to the meeting assistant; an explicit LMA ask is told to wait for LMA start. An answer that becomes ready while the VP is posting another message (a help or mode reply, say) can interleave with it on any platform; it is most likely on Webex and Chime, which post by typing.
  • The VP never answers itself. Its answers begin Live Meeting Assistant, which cannot be read as a question; its configured introduction, recording-start, recording-stop and exit messages are recognised and ignored; and messages from the VP’s own display name are ignored.
  • The Virtual Participant logs each question and answer as [chat-ask] question ... and [chat-ask] answered platform=<platform> chars=<n> messages=<n> ms=<n>, without the question text. The meeting-assistant function’s own log records the request it receives, question included, as it does for every meeting-assistant request.

Meeting Chat in the Transcript

Section titled “Meeting Chat in the Transcript”

The VP adds the messages participants send to everyone in the meeting chat to the meeting’s transcript, on Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet. Each message becomes one transcript line on its own channel, MEETING_CHAT, with the sender’s display name as the speaker (or Unknown sender where the platform does not show one) and the message as it was typed. Its time is the point in the meeting at which the VP read the message, on the same clock as the speech: no platform gives a message time that can be placed on the recording’s timeline. A link in a message whose address is not in its text is added after the text as (link: <address>). The meeting page shows these lines among the speech, labelled · chat, as plain text with any web address shown as code to copy rather than as a link; see Meeting chat messages.

It is on by default. Turn it off with Virtual Participant › Add the meeting chat to the transcript on the Configuration page; like the section’s other settings, a change applies to the next Virtual Participant to start.

What is not added:

  • the LMA chat commands, whatever state the meeting is in (LMA start typed while the meeting is already running is still a command). An LMA ask or @LMA question is added, since it was asked in front of everyone, and so is the meeting assistant’s answer the VP posts to it, once, under the VP’s display name. The VP’s notices in reply to a question (asked too soon, still answering another, too long, no answer from the assistant) are not added. An answer is added only when its question was: the answer to a question that was not added (sent privately, say, or while the transcript was paused) is not, nor is one that arrives after LMA pause;
  • replies to a consent notice, and everything the VP itself posts (its introduction, status messages and help, and the answers as the chat panel shows them, which are added once as above);
  • private messages: on Zoom (the browser join and the Meeting SDK join), a message sent to anyone but everyone (on a meeting whose chat continues in a Team Chat group chat, a message to Meeting Group Chat is sent to everyone), and a message whose recipient the VP could not read (a chat label in a language other than English, or a Meeting SDK message that names no receiver). Only a message the VP read as sent to everyone is added. Teams and Google Meet meeting chats have no private messages;
  • what the chat panel already held when the VP first read it (the Teams browser join reads the whole panel once it is in), and anything sent while the transcript is paused (LMA pause) or while consent is withheld. Those messages are not kept for later: resuming does not add them;
  • everything while Amazon Transcribe › Redact personal information is on, whatever the meeting’s language, because chat text is not redacted. If the VP could not read the Configuration page’s values when it started, it does not know the saved choice and adds no chat for that meeting (outcome=redaction-unknown; the [SETTINGS] line at start says why the values were not applied);
  • more than 60 messages a minute, or 3,000 in a meeting;
  • edits and deletions made after a message was sent: the line keeps the text the VP first read;
  • the chat of Webex and Amazon Chime meetings. The Webex chat the VP reads does not reliably show which messages are private, so Webex is left out until it can be told apart. The VP still reads Webex chat for the chat commands, and writes the debug line below for it with outcome=not-covered, so a live meeting shows whether a private message can be recognised (recipient=direct).

Chat lines are stored with the meeting’s transcript, expire with it (Days to keep transcript segments), and are deleted with it, including by Delete the transcript after the summary.

The meeting summary uses them. Each message is part of the transcript the summary sections read, as a line beginning [chat], and a meeting with chat gets a CHAT & SHARED LINKS section: what was discussed in the chat, and the links participants shared, listed from the messages themselves rather than written by the model. See Chat and Shared Links. The summary is kept when the transcript is deleted, so the links it lists outlive the chat lines they came from. The copy of the transcript written for the knowledge base’s meeting-history search leaves the chat lines out, though its copy of the summary includes the chat section. Chat lines are not used, in this release, by the meeting assistant, sentiment, speaker naming or the wake phrase: those read what was said.

Meeting Consent

Section titled “Meeting Consent”

When the deployment’s consent mode (the Meeting Consent section of the Configuration page), or the Meeting consent choice made on the Create Virtual Participant form, is Notify or Opt-in, the VP posts a notice in the meeting chat once it is admitted and reads the replies typed in the chat and, unless the Meeting Consent setting Read spoken replies is off, those said aloud and addressed to LMA (“LMA, I consent”, “LMA, I object”). In Notify it records and transcribes from the start, and stops at once when a participant objects. In Opt-in it joins with capture withheld (no transcript, audio or screen recording, voice assistant or chat added to the transcript); with spoken replies read, the meeting audio still goes to the meeting’s transcription engine, only so that a reply said aloud can be heard, and nothing else heard is kept (see Spoken replies). It starts only when every participant present has consented, in the chat or aloud, or when the meeting’s owner has confirmed in LMA that everyone agreed; until then its detail line reads Waiting for consent: … and LMA start is refused. It keeps screenshots of the chat and the participant list as evidence when consent is granted and on every objection, and can be set to leave the meeting after an objection. With mode Off it behaves as before, apart from a short read of its own row before joining, where a per-meeting choice is stored; see Choosing the mode for one meeting.

A reply is the whole chat message, as for the commands above: the configured consent and objection phrases (by default “I consent”, “I agree”, “I accept”, and “I object”, “I do not consent”, “stop recording” and others), or LMA consent, LMA no consent and LMA object. Said aloud, a reply counts only when it is addressed to LMA: “LMA, I consent” or “LMA consent”, and “LMA, I object” or “LMA, please stop recording”. Which platforms support which rule, how the participants present are counted, and what is stored are in Meeting Consent › Virtual Participant. While the meeting runs a consent flow, LMA help adds a line naming LMA consent and LMA no consent.

Troubleshooting

Section titled “Troubleshooting”
  • Scheduling issues: Check the Step Functions execution logs in the AWS console for errors related to meeting scheduling and trigger timing.
  • Joining or streaming issues: Check ECS Fargate or EC2 task logs in CloudWatch for errors during the browser launch, meeting join, or audio streaming phases.
  • VP stuck “in progress”: This may indicate that the ECS task crashed unexpectedly. This issue was identified and fixed in v0.3.0. Ensure you are running the latest version.
